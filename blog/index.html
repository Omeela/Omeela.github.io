Notes on making things for a small screen, from a bakery website to a fairy tale that fits in a minute.

Notebook entries

The recipe at the stoveNovember 26, 2026

The onions are in the pan. The recipe on your phone says “add the stock.” How much? The ingredient list is three screens up, your thumb is oily, and the onions have begun to stick.

A recipe page gets used twice. At the store, a shopper needs one list with everything on it. At the stove, a cook needs the amount beside the action. Keep “2 cups vegetable stock” on the shopping list. If the stock goes in twice, let the steps do the arithmetic. “Pour ½ cup stock into the hot pan and scrape up the browned bits.” Later, “Add the remaining 1½ cups stock with the beans.”

To test the page, take a screenshot of just the middle step on a phone. Hand it to someone with an empty measuring cup. If they can point to the line they’d fill it to, that step can stand on its own.

One Inch of RainNovember 25, 2026

The rain at booth seven cost a penny an inch. Most people held out flowerpots. A boy in a wedding suit put his head under the spout.

“Your collar,” said Nell, who ran the booth.

“That’s the idea.” He fed a penny into the slot. His hair went flat. A drop hung from each ear. Across the fairground, a photographer lowered her camera.

A bride came running, holding her skirt in both fists. The boy shut his eyes. Nell reached for the towel she kept under the till.

“Did it work?” the bride asked.

The boy opened one eye. “Did you see her take any more pictures?”

His sister considered this. Then she took the pins out of her veil and set them on Nell’s counter, one by one. “I’ve been smiling since breakfast,” she said. “Have you got another penny?”

Nell turned the little brass key that opened the booth for cleaning. Water fell in a square the size of a kitchen table. By the time the photographer caught up, the bride and her brother were floating the veil in a puddle, trying to get a beetle aboard.

“That one,” the bride said, without looking up.

The poster in the phoneNovember 24, 2026

The fox on the book fair poster wears round glasses. On a library wall, you can see the tiny price tag hanging from one ear. On the library website, the fox is still charming, but the line about which entrance to use has shrunk to the width of your thumbnail.

Keep the illustration. Under it, give the event its own heading, “Autumn Book Fair,” followed by two short lines of ordinary page text. “Saturday, 10 a.m. to 2 p.m.” Then, “Enter through the courtyard on Elm Street.” Someone meeting a friend can now copy the directions into a message, and the words can wrap as the reader enlarges the type.

Try a small editing exercise before publishing. Cover the picture and ask a friend to tell you where to stand at ten on Saturday. If they head for the library’s front desk, check which detail you left inside the fox.

The UnderstudyNovember 23, 2026

The dragon split from chin to belly ten minutes before curtain. All six children inside it stood up. From the front row came the voice of somebody’s little brother. “I can see Owen’s socks.”

There was green thread in the costume box, but the needle had gone home in a pirate’s cuff. Bea, who played the mountain, took off her brown blanket and laid it over the tear. It reached from the dragon’s shoulders to the tip of its tail.

“Now what do I climb?” whispered the knight.

Bea looked at the wooden sword in his hand. “You could knock.”

When the curtain went up, the knight tapped twice on the floor. A green snout poked out from under the blanket. The children had forgotten to agree on who would speak. Six sleepy voices said “Come in,” one after another, and the whole front row took off their shoes.

The knight took his off too. Bea lifted a corner of the blanket to let him in. For the rest of the play, the sword lay by the door with the shoes.

What happens when I tap?November 22, 2026

A pottery studio has one place left in its Saturday class. You pick a seat, type your name, and reach a button labeled “Continue.” You take your thumb off the screen. Will there be a chance to check the price? Have you just promised to pay?

Write the button from the other side of the tap. If the next screen shows the order, call the button “Review booking.” On that screen, put “Saturday, 10 a.m. · One seat · $35” above a button labeled “Pay $35 and book.” After payment succeeds, give the reader something worth taking a screenshot of: “Your seat is booked for Saturday at 10 a.m.” Include the studio address underneath.

Try this with a paper sketch. Cover the next screen and ask someone what they expect the button to do. Then uncover it. If they expected to see a receipt and you show them another form, you’ve found the exact word or step to revisit.

The Bell Mender’s ReceiptNovember 21, 2026

When the school bell lost its voice, the bell mender sent her apprentice up the tower with a teaspoon. “Scrape out whatever’s caught under the lip.”

Kit came down with half a spoonful of Tuesday. It smelled of wet mittens. Inside it, very faintly, a boy was sounding out the word chrysanthemum. Each time he reached the second syllable, the bell rang and he had to start again.

The mender fetched the school register. The boy was eighty now. They found him selling daffodils outside the station. Kit held out the spoon. He put down his change, shut his eyes and finished the word.

Across town the school bell rang. The florist wrapped three stems in brown paper for the repair bill. On the receipt, in the space marked time spent, the mender wrote “One Tuesday” and let him keep the spoon.

The bakery homepage test

Imagine standing outside a bakery in the rain, phone in one hand. You want to know whether the shop is open and whether there’s any cinnamon bread left. That little scene makes a surprisingly good design brief.

Sketch the first screen with just the bakery name, today’s hours, and a link to the menu. Put prices beside the names so a reader can scan one row. A photograph earns its space when it answers a question, like whether the cinnamon bread comes as a loaf or a single roll.

Then hand the page to someone and ask, “Could you pick something for breakfast and find the shop?” Watch where their thumb goes first. The pause before their first tap can tell you more about the layout than asking whether they like the colors.

A fairy tale in three pictures

A girl finds a moonbeam caught on her clothesline. By morning it will fade. She could keep it in a jar, but the moths have already gathered at her window.

For a short video, that opening gives us three pictures to work with. First, show the thread of light snagged on a wooden peg. Next, let the glass jar fill the frame as the girl lowers it over the light. Finally, show her lifting the jar again as the moths fly out carrying silver dust.

The middle picture matters most. Without the jar, the girl has nothing to give up. With it, opening her hand tells the ending before the narrator needs to say a word.
